# Costs, not signals, decide 0DTE outcomes

Retail traders and developers automate 0DTE mostly as defined-risk premium selling (iron condors, iron butterflies, credit spreads), and the evidence says the buyers on the other side lose on average. The best academic dataset, Cboe SPX trades from January 2021 to September 2023, shows retail losing about **$241,000 per day** net of fees (**$350,000 per day** once daily expiries began in May 2022). Roughly 60% of the loss is the spread paid to market makers, and the split is stark: debit (long) orders lost **$364,000 per day** while credit orders *earned* **$122,000 per day** ([Beckmeyer, Branger and Gayda, Dec 2023 version](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)). The 0DTE variance risk premium is real but small and fat-tailed. A 2026 correction to the main strategy-level backtest flipped its net-of-cost result to "no strategy or basket retains a positive net Sharpe ratio" ([Vilkov repository, KNOWN-ISSUES](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/KNOWN-ISSUES.md)). Published positive track records come from vendors, mid-price backtests or self-reports; I found no audited multi-year record of an automated retail 0DTE strategy. For your tool, this means the +2-3% of premium that your morning signals add over a coin flip is a plausible but unproven edge. It is the same order of magnitude as the round-trip spread, and a blind long 0DTE buyer is expected to lose. The highest-value next steps are measuring real bid/ask costs, testing structure changes against your current long singles as paired comparisons, and keeping the number of new looks small.

## Automation clusters around no-code bots, and the products sell win rates

Retail automation sits in three layers. No-code bot platforms with built-in 0DTE backtesters (Option Alpha, Option Omega) dominate, developers use broker-API stacks (QuantConnect, IBKR, TradeStation, Alpaca), and paid signal or data services (Tachyon-style condor signals, QuantedOptions, SpotGamma) sit on top. Option Alpha's own study of roughly 230,000 closed 0DTE trades found SPY was **81%** of trades, QQQ second, and iron butterflies the most popular structure, with neutral strategies about twice as common as directional credit spreads ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/0dte-options-strategy-performance)). Cboe reports that **over 95%** of SPX 0DTE trades are limited-risk, with naked shorts about 4%, and estimates retail at 50-60% of SPX 0DTE volume ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/0-dt-es-decoded-positioning-trends-and-market-impact)). Cboe is the exclusive SPX lister, so read its classification with that interest in mind. Academic estimates put retail at only 2-4% of all SPX volume and above 6% of 0DTE, but they identify retail only through a price-improvement auction for orders of up to 10 contracts, so the two measures are not reconciled ([Beckmeyer et al.](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)).

The families and their evidence grades differ sharply. Short premium shows the most attractive headline numbers. Option Alpha reports iron butterflies winning 66.8% and condors 70.2% among profitable traders with more than 100 trades, but that is a survivorship-conditioned subsample: only **49.64%** of all 0DTE users were profitable, and Tuesday, Thursday and Friday trades were net negative overall ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/0dte-options-strategy-performance)). The Tachyon signal service advertises a 78.9% win rate and Sharpe 2.32, but its backtest omits slippage by its own disclaimer, its filters were fitted on the same sample, and its live record is days long ([Tachyon](https://futures.aeromir.com/tachyon)). A high win rate is arithmetic, not evidence: Tachyon's average credit of $1.26 on a $5-wide condor risks about $3.74 to make $1.26, and Cboe's own example condor risks $9 to make $1 ([Cboe](https://www.cboe.com/insights/posts/henry-schwartzs-zero-day-spx-iron-condor-strategy-a-deep-dive/)). Self-reported accounts exist (a practitioner claiming roughly 30% annual returns and an $800,000 drawdown from iron flies, per [Theta Profits](https://www.thetaprofits.com/0dte-iron-flies-dale-perryman-on-4m-in-profits-and-an-800k-drawdown/)), but none shows broker statements, and reader comments on a companion article report net-negative backtests ([Theta Profits](https://www.thetaprofits.com/the-maria-shoe-trade-how-a-0dte-iron-butterfly-paid-for-1500-shoes-again-and-again/)).

Directional and momentum approaches, your family, have academic backing for the underlying return pattern but not for the options trade. Opening-range-breakout papers by Zarattini and co-authors report large results on QQQ and SPY shares, but the headline Sharpe of 1.33 requires volatility scaling with leverage up to 4x, the unscaled version made only 6.2% a year (Sharpe 0.61), and the authors sell research and trading education ([Zarattini, Aziz and Barbon](https://alexandria.unisg.ch/server/api/core/bitstreams/a99aba00-f967-49b3-aceb-f544dc386e0b/content)). An independent replication on index CFDs (a blog by a vendor of trading products, so treat it as informative rather than authoritative) reproduced the gross edge on Nasdaq futures at about +0.13 R per trade, but after spread and slippage the result was roughly zero for Nasdaq and negative for SPX; the first-candle direction beat random direction by only about 0.1 R, about one round-trip cost ([MQL5 replication](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). LLM and "AI" 0DTE agents exist only as hackathon projects with no published P&L; the typical pattern keeps a deterministic engine in charge of strikes and sizing and limits the model to veto or size-down gating ([lablab.ai](https://lablab.ai/submissions/jnj5lhf3bm7jz19u796yp5ct)). Broader LLM-trading studies on stocks find that advantages shrink over longer windows and wider universes ([FINSABER](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.07078)).

## Buyers lose to the spread while sellers collect a thin premium

The Beckmeyer et al. paper is the only retail P&L study I found, and its key decomposition matters for your tool. Using mid-quotes, cumulative retail loss is about **$30 million**; using actual transaction prices it exceeds **$125 million**, so over $90 million is spread cost, and gross P&L flattened after November 2022 while net kept falling ([Beckmeyer et al.](https://wp.lancs.ac.uk/fofi2024/files/2024/04/FoFI-2024-146-Leander-Gayda.pdf)). Retail effective spreads were **6.0% on calls and 5.0% on puts**, already about half the 12.5% and 9.6% that non-retail traders paid. Buyers lost money and sellers made money in every hour of the expiry day, including the 10:00 hour, and single-leg trades lost on average (calls -9.4% and puts -5.6% of posted margin) while median put and call spreads earned about +3% margin-adjusted. Aggregate results swing hard by month, from +$286,000 per day in January 2022 to -$680,000 in September 2022. An earlier version of the paper reported smaller totals ($184,000 per day, more than $70 million; see [BNN Bloomberg](https://www.bnnbloomberg.ca/day-traders-lose-358-000-per-day-gambling-on-zero-day-options-1.1910634)), and it remains a working paper, not a journal article. Two cautions apply to using it as a verdict on your signals. The data are SPX, not SPY or QQQ, and ETF options carry much tighter ATM spreads; and the aggregate loss is spread across thousands of mostly non-systematic traders, so it describes the average buyer rather than a conditioned signal.

The seller side is real but small. Vilkov's tests of static SPX strategies held from 10:00 to the close found a statistically detectable variance risk premium, but wide, skewed return distributions, mostly insignificant mean returns, and P&L driven by the index's realized skewness ([Hull Tactical summary](https://hulltactical.com/2026/07/08/0dte-options-tiny-edge-huge-distribution/)). The corrected repository numbers are the sobering part: the put ratio spread's Sharpe went from **+1.06 at mid to -0.61 net**, the risk reversal from +0.65 to +0.10, the bear put spread from +0.48 to -0.73, and iron butterflies and condors to -2.67 ([Vilkov repository](https://github.com/vilkovgr/0dte-strategies/blob/main/KNOWN-ISSUES.md)). The error that caused this was a units bug: the half-spread had been charged at about 0.022 basis points instead of about 2.2. A second independent study of SPX 0DTE (2020-2024) finds large risk premia in prices but economically insignificant volatility-arbitrage Sharpe ratios after costs and estimation risk, concluding the options are "expensive but do not appear to be mispriced" ([HKUST](https://bm.hkust.edu.hk/bizinsight/2026/04/are-0dte-options-mispriced)). A vendor study of 193 SPY Wednesdays found realized moves about 13% below implied, which is the same order as the spread ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/are-0dte-straddles-overpriced-193-spy-sessions-data-study)). The 0DTE edge for sellers exists on paper and is consumed by costs and tail days unless execution is good.

The evidence for your directional premise is thinner than the headlines suggest. Market intraday momentum is documented, but it is a **closing-half-hour effect**: the first half-hour or rest-of-day return predicts the last 30 minutes with in-sample R-squared of roughly 1.5-3% ([Gao et al. summary](https://alphaarchitect.com/2014/08/attention-prop-traders-the-first-half-hour-of-trading-predicts-the-last-half-hour/); [Baltussen et al., JFE 2021](https://www3.nd.edu/~zda/intramom.pdf)). In Baltussen's pooled equity-index futures table the first-half-hour predictor had a *negative* out-of-sample R-squared (-1.71%), while rest-of-day return worked (about +2.9%), and the effect is concentrated on days when dealers are net short gamma. Neither paper tests an options payoff, and no source I found tests morning signals with 0DTE option payoffs net of option spreads. Dealer-gamma evidence pulls in several directions: the 0DTE-specific papers find 0DTE gamma does not propagate volatility, and one finds higher dealer gamma lowers volatility over about the next hour ([Quantpedia summary of Adams et al.](https://quantpedia.com/do-sp500-0dtes-options-increase-market-volatility/)), while every claim of a tradable GEX edge I found comes from a vendor selling the signal.

## Backtests flip sign when fills and data resolution get realistic

No study measures backtest-to-live decay for retail 0DTE systematically, but the indirect evidence is consistent. A community replication of a tastylive iron-fly study on 1-minute data got a 55% win rate and -$8 average, against the claimed ~70% and +$61, with commenters blaming 10-minute data and mid-price fills ([Option Alpha community](https://app.optionalpha.com/community/posts/0-dte-backtest-tasty-gives-completely--202508077174); a search-level summary, single source). A user reported a bot built from a backtest struggling to close trades live ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/bots/0dte-research-insights-bot)), and Option Alpha itself says daily SPY and QQQ expiries from late 2022 slowed 0DTE profits and that a 3-year backtest is regime-dependent ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/0dte-options-strategy-performance)). Cost scale matters: assuming 25% mid-to-bid slippage, daily entry and exit on Nasdaq-100 0DTE cost about **300-350 basis points a year**, and exiting expiring in-the-money options alone cost over 350 bps ([Volos](https://volossoftware.com/insights/improving-0dte-trading-returns); vendor). The reasonable prior is that any positive 0DTE backtest that omits slippage is near zero or negative until live-tested.

The most common failure modes follow from this. Fill assumptions come first: thinkorswim paperMoney fills multi-leg orders at mid and ignores size ([Investing Daily](https://investingdaily.com/paper-trading)), TradersPost paper defaults to midpoint ([TradersPost](https://blog.traderspost.io/article/paper-trading-vs-live-results)), Alpaca's free options feed is indicative and 15 minutes delayed and staff call it a debugging feed ([Alpaca forum](https://forum.alpaca.markets/t/what-is-the-indicative-pricing-feed-for-options/14595)), and IBKR paper accounts get delayed US options data unless they share a funded live account's subscription ([IBKR](https://www.interactivebrokers.com/en/trading/papertrader-delayed-data.php)). Your 15-minute quote lag, which inflated paper P&L by about $32 per trade, is therefore a textbook instance of the documented pattern, not a one-off bug. Second, the multiple-testing problem: QuantedOptions advertises a best win rate of 84.6% chosen from 1,540 configurations ([QuantedOptions](https://www.quantedoptions.com)), while Harvey and Liu show Sharpe ratios must be haircut nonlinearly for the number of tests tried ([Harvey and Liu](https://people.duke.edu/~charvey/Research/Published_Papers/P120_Backtesting.PDF)). Third, tail days. On August 5, 2024 the VIX had its largest one-day spike on record and wide option spreads fed the VIX itself ([BIS Bulletin 95](https://www.bis.org/publ/bisbull95.htm)), and on April 9, 2025 SPY closed +8.72% against a 10:00 implied move of 3.51%, about 2.5x ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/are-0dte-straddles-overpriced-193-spy-sessions-data-study)). One practitioner reported stops triggered by a +10.39% open-to-high move and then "crazy fills" when stop orders executed as market orders, and moved to stop-limit orders ([Early Retirement Now](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2026/01/30/options-trading-series-part-14-year-2025-review/)). Mid-price backtests are least accurate on exactly these days, because spreads blow out when stops trigger.

## What serious practitioners converge on, and how little of it is proven

Practitioner guidance converges on a short list: enter after the opening volatility (about 10:00-10:30 ET, or mid-day after a spike), take profits early, be wary of tight stops, keep risk defined and small, and skip or downsize FOMC and similar event days. Option Alpha's profitable traders enter around **10:15** and exit just after 12:00, with an example rule of +15% target, -25% stop and a 12:00 exit ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/0dte-options-strategy-performance)); a tastylive test of 72 condor management variants reportedly found about a 25% profit target gave the best balance, with win rates barely moving ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/0-dte-breakdown-legging-out-in-0-dte-iron-condors-part-2-03-30-2026); search summary only). A secondary stop-loss study reports that win rates fall below 50% with a 0.5x-credit stop and that roughly 48% of positions stopped at that level would have recovered by expiry ([StockWireX](https://stockwirex.com/?p=47372); low-quality secondary source). On sizing, Saxo suggests about 1% of the account per trade, up to 5% for small accounts ([Saxo](https://home.saxo/content/articles/options/trading-0dtes---get-your-feet-wet---without-drowning---part-2-07092023)), and Early Retirement Now says to size so that a repeat of April 2025 is survivable. None of these is demonstrated net of costs on independent data; the entry-time "consensus" ranges from 09:35 to 15:51 across sources, so it is convention, not a measured optimum.

Where practitioner numbers exist, they cluster around modest capture: the same Early Retirement Now review reports 2025 0DTE puts keeping **62.2%** of premium and calls **53.1%**, with April's gains "largely given back" ([Early Retirement Now](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2026/01/30/options-trading-series-part-14-year-2025-review/)). Day-of-week and event filters are mostly asserted: Option Alpha's template ships FOMC, CPI and weekday skip switches with a VIX cap but no published results ([Option Alpha template](https://optionalpha.com/bots/0dte-iron-condor-w-iron-fly-hedging)), and a tastylive claim of a 23-for-23 post-FOMC entry is too small a sample to conclude from. The best-supported practices are therefore the unglamorous ones the independent literature backs: defined risk, small size, limit orders, and avoiding event-day gaps, rather than any particular delta or entry minute.

## Seven ideas ranked for your morning-family lab, and what to skip

Start with a candid reading of where you stand. The academic data say the average 0DTE *buyer* loses, and your morning family is a buyer: it pays the spread twice, pays theta, and holds the long-vol side of a market where sellers earn the variance risk premium. Your simulation's finding that even the blind trade looks positive is, in light of the Beckmeyer result, a warning sign about the pricing model (Black-Scholes with IV = k x VIX likely underprices real 0DTE options) rather than a gift; the relative edge over the coin-flip null is the only trustworthy number there. That relative edge, +2-3% of premium at t about 3, resembles the independent replication of opening-range breakouts, where first-candle direction beat random by about 0.1 R, roughly one round-trip cost, and the 2021-2026 period was at best marginally positive ([MQL5 replication](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/776235)). Your "fading since 2025" pattern fits that. It does not prove the trade loses; it does mean the absolute net level is the open question, and the literature says to expect it to be negative until shown otherwise.

| Rank | Idea | Why the literature points here | Test design | Prior |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Measure real spreads and slippage (IBKR real-time chain snapshots, ask-for-buys/bid-for-sells fill model, stress-day stop slippage) | Spread is the dominant, persistent loss channel; paper and delayed feeds are documented optimistic | Log bid/ask for your exact strikes at entry and exit each trading day; compare to your simulated 1-cent assumption | Near-certain value |
| 2 | Debit vertical in the signal direction vs long single | Multi-leg retail trades outperform singles; your +50% target already truncates upside, so a capped payoff costs less than it seems | Paired shadow arm on identical signal days | Moderate; no independent head-to-head exists |
| 3 | Credit spread in the signal direction (sell the put spread after an up-drive) | Only consistent buyer/seller asymmetry in the data: credits +$122,000/day vs debits -$364,000/day | Shadow arm plus a coin-flip-direction credit-spread null to separate premium from signal | Moderate; tail-risk dominated |
| 4 | One pre-registered VIX-open split | Zarattini's edge is concentrated in high-VIX days (conflicted source) | Two buckets, fixed threshold, descriptive only | Low |
| 5 | One price-based gamma-regime split (5-day RSI proxy) | Only cheap, no-new-data test of the dealer-gamma hypothesis | One split, fixed in advance | Low |
| 6 | Entry-price richness (10:00 implied move vs trailing realized move) | Beckmeyer: expensive options meant larger retail losses, but only before May 2022 | Record the 10:00 straddle price as a covariate | Low |
| 7 | LLM gating as a veto filter | Hackathon pattern only; no evidence of benefit | Count as a filter; require a null | Very low |

**Measure costs first.** Every other test is uninterpretable without it, because the net level is what decides whether the strategy is worth trading. Record the actual bid and ask of the strikes you would trade at the decision time and at each exit, at one-minute granularity if the feed allows. Compute the ratio of round-trip spread to expected move per structure, since that is the number that decides between a single and a spread. Vendor figures for ATM SPY 0DTE spreads run from 1-2 cents ([Saxo](https://home.saxo/content/articles/options/trading-0dtes---get-your-feet-wet---without-drowning---part-2-07092023)) to $0.02-0.05 ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/0dte-spy-complete-intraday-playbook-same-day-options)), while the only SPY/QQQ spread sample I found measured 5.8% of premium near the close on one day ([Schaeffer's](https://www.schaeffersresearch.com/content/bgs/2023/09/22/0dte-options-are-higher-spreads-imminent)). Your simulation's 1 cent sits at the optimistic end, and at an assumed $2 premium a 2-5 cent round trip is 1-2.5% of premium, which is comparable to your +2-3% edge. Model stop exits with wider slippage than entries, since stops trigger when spreads are widest, and add a unit test on cost magnitudes of the kind Vilkov's repository added after its half-spread bug. When real-time IBKR data arrives, confirm that the paper account is receiving real-time rather than delayed quotes.

**Test structure with paired, not independent, comparisons.** Run a debit vertical (long the ATM strike, short one or two strikes out) and, separately, a signal-direction credit spread as shadow arms on *the same days and signals* as your long single. The paired difference removes day-level noise and so needs far fewer trades than testing each arm against its own coin-flip null. Debit spreads pay two half-spreads at entry but cut theta and vega exposure, and your existing +50% cap on long singles suggests the second leg gives up little (my inference; the literature has no head-to-head test). The credit variant is the strongest literature-backed direction, but it moves you to the side that earns the variance premium *and* exposes you to tail days like April 9, 2025, so evaluate it by drawdown, worst day and a bootstrap, not t-statistics alone, since returns are heavily skewed. Run the coin-flip-direction credit spread as its own null, because a credit spread can earn money from the premium alone, and without that arm you cannot tell premium capture from signal. Even the credit evidence is weak after costs: Vilkov's corrected net Sharpes for credit-style structures are negative, and the one retail credit result is net of retail price improvement that you may not get.

**Limit the number of looks.** Every extra arm, filter, entry window or target level is a multiple-testing draw. Pre-register the exact structures, strikes, entry rules and metrics before looking at data, and keep the total at the three or four top rows in the table above. As an inference from standard multiple-comparison arithmetic, not a finding from the notes: with four new tests, a Bonferroni-style threshold is t of about 2.5 instead of 2, and Harvey and Liu's result supports penalizing marginal results most heavily. Also reconsider what your 40-day rule can tell you. As an illustration with an assumed per-trade standard deviation of about 40% of premium (your +50%/-35% bounds make this plausible but I have not measured it), a +2.5% edge needs on the order of **1,000 trades** for t = 2, so 40 days of a few trades each cannot confirm an edge of that size. It can still falsify a strategy that is clearly losing on real fills. Treat the 40-day rule as a loss detector and a cost-measurement window, and treat any "positive net" result in it as provisional. The same applies to your decade of simulation: it is a stronger test of the *relative* signal than of profitability.

**What the literature says is unlikely to help.** More tuning of entry minute, strike distance, target and stop levels is the classic overfitting route; the practitioner "consensus" is convention, and a stop study suggests tighter stops mostly lower win rate. Your finding that nothing passed after 11:30 and that no calendar slice broke the signals is consistent with the literature's mostly anecdotal event-day claims, so add no more calendar filters; event-day straddles are reportedly priced at roughly double neighboring weeks, which argues for downsizing rather than expecting extra expectancy ([FlashAlpha](https://flashalpha.com/articles/are-0dte-straddles-overpriced-193-spy-sessions-data-study)). The closing-half-hour momentum effect is real but tiny (R-squared of 1-3%) and spreads widen into the close, so monetizing it with 0DTE options is unlikely to beat costs. Commercial GEX signals have no independent out-of-sample support, and your composite signal already found no edge. LLM gating has no evidence behind it.

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question from "is my signal good?" to "does the trade survive its costs?" Retail 0DTE losses are largely a spread and direction-of-structure problem, not a prediction problem: mid-priced gross losses were a fraction of net losses, and the sign of the structure (debit versus credit) mattered more than any measured signal quality. A morning-drive signal with a small, decaying relative edge is the kind of finding that independent replications typically shrink to roughly one round-trip cost. Your own work has already caught two of the standard failure modes (delayed-quote optimism and unmeasured costs), which is more than most published retail systems do.

The most useful outcome in the next month is probably a measured cost number and a clear answer on structure, not a better signal. If real-time spreads show round-trip costs near or above your +2-3% edge, the right conclusion is that the long-single morning family does not clear costs, and the paired credit-versus-debit shadow arms become the only avenues worth the sample. If costs come in well below the edge, the family earns a longer live test sized for roughly a thousand trades. Either way, the evidence here has limits: nearly all of the academic work is on SPX, not SPY or QQQ; the only retail P&L study ends in September 2023; and several key papers could be read only through abstracts or secondary summaries. Nothing in the literature I found supports the claim that a retail 0DTE buyer, automated or not, earns a net edge.
